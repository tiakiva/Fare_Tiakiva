🌴 The Coconut Tree, Nourishing Plant and Cultural Symbol

The coconut tree is one of the fifty agricultural plants considered crucial for humanity and listed in the annex of the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources.

In French Polynesia, coconut groves occupy three-quarters of the usable agricultural land and represent about one-third of farms. Copra is the main product from coconut plantations, making it a “social” activity strongly supported by the government. Traditional coconut varieties are generally classified into four types:

1. The Tall Type

These are often referred to simply as “Tall.” They usually form cross-pollinating populations (mostly allogamous) and are quite heterogeneous. Talls grow at least 50 cm per year when young. They generally start flowering between 5 and 10 years after planting, with an economic lifespan estimated at 60–70 years.

2. The Self-Pollinating Dwarf Types

These prefer self-fertilization. Often called Malay Dwarfs, Fragile Dwarfs, or Thin-Trunk Dwarfs. They grow 15 to 30 cm per year when young, have a productive lifespan of 30 to 40 years, and usually begin flowering 12 to 30 months after being planted.

3. The Compact Dwarf Type

Specific to the Pacific region, they are described as cross-pollinating (allogamous). These dwarfs have a thick trunk, often starting with a marked basal bulge, and grow around 7 to 12 cm per year. They are rare and insufficiently collected.

4. The "Semi-Tall" Type

These are intermediate between Dwarfs and Talls or Compact Dwarfs and Talls. They seem to be more common than previously thought, but were somewhat overlooked by scientists who preferred a simple two-category model: Dwarfs and Talls. In Coconut Varieties and Forms in French Polynesia, DAG

🌿 Therapeutic Use

Coconut is a true ally for boosting the immune system thanks to its richness in copper and iron (needed for red blood cells), and lauric acid — a fatty acid also found in breast milk — known for its antiviral, antibacterial, and antifungal properties.

Monoi, the traditional oil used for the body and hair, is used by Polynesians as a cosmetic and massage oil. Monoi is made from coconut oil.

🌺 The Legend of the Coconut Tree

Did you know that drinking water from a coconut is like kissing an eel? Hear the legend of Hina, whose hand was promised to an eel. At sixteen, the beautiful princess of Pape’uriri, Hina, is promised in marriage to the prince of Lake Vaihiria, Faaravaianuu. To her shock, her suitor is a monstrous eel. Terrified, Hina flees to Vaira’o, on the Tai’arapu peninsula, to the hero Maui. The eel, i.e., Prince Faaravaianuu, leaves the lake and follows her. Maui places two stone tiki statues on the cliff and manages to catch the creature. He cuts it into three parts and wraps the head in tapa cloth before handing it to Hina. “Do not set this bundle down until you're home. Plant it in the center of your marae — it holds great treasures.” On her way home, Hina puts it down to bathe and forgets Maui’s advice. The earth swallows the eel's head, and from it sprouts a strange plant, growing into a towering tree: the first coconut tree (tumu ha’ari). The dry season comes, but the coconut tree alone thrives. People drink its sweet water and see three dark spots on the fruit — eyes and a mouth: the face of the eel. To this day, flying over the Vaihiria Valley, the river appears like the winding path of an eel — the journey of Faaravaianuu to reclaim Hina. So drinking coconut water, like Hina, is to taste a royal kiss once refused. From Tahiti Héritage
