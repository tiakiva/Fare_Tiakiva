Tamanu

By Fare Tiakiva

🌿 Tamanu – Sacred Healing Tree

Scientific Name: Calophyllum inophyllum

Family: Calophyllaceae

Origin: Coastal regions of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

📜 Explanatory Text

The tāmanu is a widespread tree in the Pacific, commonly found along seashores. In Tahiti, it is less abundant than before due to intensive logging. Nevertheless, it is still well established on certain Polynesian islands where its seeds are collected. Formerly, Polynesians called this majestic tree 'ati', now known as tāmanu. Its botanical name is "Calophyllum inophyllum". The Calophyllum genus includes 187 species found throughout the Pacific and Indian Oceans. This species ranges from East Africa to the easternmost part of Polynesia, with peak density in Indonesia. The floating fruits explain its natural dispersal via ocean currents. Today, tāmanu is present in all archipelagos, especially in the Tuamotu and Society Islands. In the Australs and Marquesas, it has become rare. It grows in coastal vegetation up to 50 m altitude, sometimes up to 200 m if planted. It prefers calcareous beaches over basaltic soils. In the past, Tahiti had vast forests of tāmanu. But in the 19th century, with the end of religious taboos and the arrival of Europeans, the trees were massively felled. The wood was highly valued for its density. The tāmanu can grow over 20 meters tall, with a trunk 2 meters in diameter. It produces fruit year-round. Its bark is cracked, brown, containing yellow latex. Its red wood is very hard. The leaves are glossy on top, matte underneath. White flowers emit a honey-like scent. The round fruit contains an oily kernel inside a hard shell. Before Christianity, the tāmanu was sacred. Planted on marae, its wood was used to carve idols. Ancient legends passed down by high priests highlight its central role. Modern scientists confirm its exceptional medicinal and cosmetic properties. Some molecules of the tāmanu are currently under pharmaceutical study. Identifying the most active Polynesian varieties could help sustainably restore lost forests. Developing a sustainable industry around tāmanu—combining planting, harvesting, and processing—could reinforce its place in Polynesian heritage. In Rangiroa, seeds are harvested in Tiputa, dried, and processed into oil. It is said that Polynesian gods seek shelter under its shade to observe humans.

🌀 Prophetic Legend of Tāmanu

In 1823, Auna-iti and Vai-au, two priests from Porapora, reported a strange event in Opoa (Raiatea) during a ritual: a gust of wind beheaded a tāmanu named Paruru-Mata’i-i-’a’ana. Everyone was astonished because this wood is extremely hard. Priest Vaita interpreted the event: "The glorious children of the trunk will come on outriggerless vessels. They will take our lands. It will mark the end of our customs, and sacred birds will mourn what this tree teaches." Another priest declared that one day women would eat the sacred foods reserved for the gods. This prediction came true with the arrival of Christianity.
