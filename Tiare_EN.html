Tiare Tahiti

🌺 Sacred, Cultural, and Medicinal Flower

Over 1,000 years ago, peoples of the Pacific Ocean, renowned for their seafaring skills, explored uninhabited territories. Sailing from west to east, the Polynesians introduced nearly 70 food and medicinal plants during their first voyages to French Polynesia, an island territory as large as Europe. Among them was the tiare tahiti (*Gardenia taitensis* – Rubiaceae), a shrub known for its large fragrant white flowers, which became one of the emblems of these islands. Both its flowers and leaves are used in Polynesian pharmacopoeia and cosmetics; you’ve likely heard of Tahiti monoi oil. Monoi is traditionally made from coconut milk or grated coconut, left in the sun for several weeks to extract its oil. During this process, *tiare tahiti* flowers (dry or fresh) are added to macerate in the oil, giving it fragrance and beneficial properties. Tiare tahiti is one of many imported plants that were propagated through layering or cuttings—techniques well mastered by these populations at the time. Although vegetative propagation is still the current method used in Polynesian horticulture, the plant is actually fruitful—a fact unknown to most Polynesians. Its native range extends from the Vanuatu Islands to Samoa, including Fiji, Tonga, Wallis & Futuna, and Niue. There, it is naturally found along coastal limestone exposed to sea winds, where some plants bear fruit enjoyed by nearby insects and some birds. In contrast, studies show that mature berries are almost never found in the Society Islands archipelago, although small sterile fruits without seed formation can be seen. Upon observation, some plants produce only female flowers, while others—called "male"—produce male flowers that under certain stress conditions (pruning, storms…) become hermaphroditic: *tiare tahiti* is thus a gynodioecious plant. The rarity of female plants in the Society archipelago is partly because "male" plants flower more abundantly and also because these flowers are used in large quantities in the region. The plant's uses led Polynesians to import only the so-called "male" plants, which have been cloned and selected for generations. To all passionate and professional horticulturists: to ensure a pure *Gardenia taitensis* genetic line, it is best to continue using traditional vegetative propagation techniques. 💠 The legend of the *tiare Maurua* (former name of Maupiti) says that *tiare tahiti* originally existed only on Maupiti, and was transported as cuttings by a young woman, Hinaraurea. The westernmost island of the Leeward Islands, Maupiti also has the most favorable climate for Gardenia growth. Just like the canoe it traveled in, the *tiare tahiti* is also a symbol of Polynesian cultural identity. Many traditions involving this plant remain a strong part of daily life in Polynesia. It holds a prominent place in Polynesian pharmacopoeia. It is no coincidence that it is cultivated around homes. Despite modern changes, *tiare tahiti* remains one of the most used plants in medicinal remedies, especially in popular remedies. Its properties are still not well known but are thought to resemble those of aspirin (anti-headache and anti-inflammatory), likely due to similar active compounds such as salicylate esters. Beyond its cosmetic value, traditional monoi (*mono’i tiare tahiti* for the Society Islands) is also used medicinally—for prevention and protection, often through massage—whether for newborns, children, or adults (fishing, hunting, dance, etc.). A daily-use product, it also comes in a wide variety of homemade preparations. Finally, this study also revealed that the creation of these objects—pillars of Polynesian cultural identity—is mostly passed down and practiced by Polynesian women.
