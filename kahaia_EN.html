Kahaia (Guettarda speciosa)

By Fare Tiakiva

🌺 Sacred Flower of the Polynesian Coast

Scientific Name: Guettarda speciosa

Family: Rubiaceae

Origin: Coastal areas of the Polynesian islands and other tropical regions

Botanical Description

  • Type: Shrub or small tree
  • Height: 2 to 6 meters
  • Leaves: Large, oval, light green, slightly fuzzy
  • Flowers: White, tubular, highly fragrant, nocturnal
  • Blooming: In the evening or at night, year-round in tropical climates

Traditional Uses

  • Ornamentation: flowers used in leis and garlands for their fragrance
  • Spirituality: plant associated with peace and serenity, used in sacred places
  • Wood: occasionally used for carving or decorative objects

Chemical Components

  • Fragrant essential oils in the flowers
  • Phenolic compounds (assumed, limited studies)

A Night Flower of Serenity

Kahaia, or the gentle fragrance of the Tuamotu (Tafano in the Society and Austral Islands or Guettarda speciosa). It is one of the most beautiful indigenous shrubs of coral beaches. Its trunk is very straight and bare almost its entire length, ending in a bouquet of large obovate leaves (oval shape with the upper part wider than the lower) and heart-shaped at the base. The flowers emit a delicate fragrance. The corolla is white, with a very long tube and a limb divided into 7 lobes. This plant has become very rare in Tahiti, but is still found in the Leeward Islands and the Tuamotu. The flowers are used to make garlands, are very fragrant, and are included in monoi oil for their scent and properties. The scent of Kahaia is said to be aphrodisiac. The leaves are used for wrapping and as plates during picnics, and the bark is used to treat rheumatism. In some archipelagos, it is used as an anti-diarrheal, fever reducer, and tonic. The wood, light, slightly pinkish with pale yellow veining, was used for making canoes, chests, and many objects. It is considered the best wood to use by friction to make fire. Sources: Useful Plants of Polynesia, Ra’au Tahiti by Paul Pétard, and the “Tahiti Héritage” website.
